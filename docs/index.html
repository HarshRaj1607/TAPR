TAPR · Checkpoint 1 · Aug 2026

Before asking how to mix a process reward,
check whether the signal works at all

An inference-only experiment on GSM8K reasoning traces, testing whether a judge that scores transitions catches broken reasoning that a judge scoring steps in isolation misses. It mostly did not, and the reasons turned out to be more interesting than the result.

My writing

01

Claim

Outcome only reward is incapable of catching poor reasoning, since models have shown to produce the right output, probably from memory, despite poor reasoning, and vice versa, right reasoning and wrong outcome. The isolated step scorer scores steps in isolation, without context, so steps can be locally correct but lead to totally poor reasoning overall. This is documented in multiple papers.

What I found was that most papers don't separate whether the signal is weak vs mixing the PRM signal with outcome reward conflicts.

TAPR rewards the transition (simplification, conclusion, substitution, implication) based on its validity. Given everything we have seen so far in the reasoning trace, does the transition follow, is it valid?

Hypothesis. The transition judge scorer catches poor reasoning much better than the isolated judge, and within outcome variance is more in the transition judge scores.

Added with Claude · the specific papers behind "most papers don't separate"

Three papers document the failure of isolated step scoring but none of them validate the signal on its own before studying how to combine it:

  • Reward Granularity in RLVR (2607.02869). Process only supervision gets the best accuracy (0.64) and the worst trace validity (0.22, below the untrained base at 0.56). They vary the mixing weight and attribute the anomaly at low process weight to conflicting optimization signals.
  • PROGRS (2604.02341). States that PRMs assign high scores to locally coherent reasoning that still ends in an incorrect answer, then builds machinery (outcome conditioned centering) to constrain the signal rather than testing it.
  • Samineni et al. (2510.18176). Finds RL improves local coherence without correctness following, using an LLM error classifier that reaches 57.8% accuracy against human annotation.

My writing

02

Methodology

Qwen2.5-3B generates 5 traces per question, started with 30 questions, and our scorers score those traces independently.

Scorers

isolated judge. Sees the question and only one step, no other content. Scores the step based only on mathematical validity.

transition. Scores the transition based on validity: no contradiction, value traceable, relevant, mathematically correct. We give the definition of valid to the judge.

First we tried using a 0 to 10 scale with different bands, low band for poor reasoning, middle band for reasoning that is valid but does not advance the problem. Found the judge doesn't use the scale well and restricts itself to either a very low or a very high score. Moved to a 0 to 1 scale with no described bands.

bare. Scores the transition just like the previous judge, but we leave the judgment of what is a valid transition to the judge and do not define it ourselves. Scale: similar, first used 0 to 10, switched to 0 to 1.

The idea to add this new scorer came after reading the interwhen paper, where I realised that the real judgement, that is what should be done vs avoided, what is the right next step, ie a reasoning step, a transition, a tool call, must lie with the agent or judge itself. In interwhen, when the human writes the policy document, they are doing the real heavy lifting when it comes to judgment and its quality, and not the agent.

Added with Claude · exact band values, and two design details

The banded prompt used four bands: 0 to 3 when any premise contradicts the problem or is fabricated or the arithmetic is wrong, 4 to 6 when premises and arithmetic are fine but the move does not advance the problem, 7 to 8 for valid and advancing with minor issues, 9 to 10 when all three checks pass cleanly.

Two things that matter for reading the results. The prefix-only property is enforced by slicing the trace in code, so a judge scoring step i is never shown step i or later, rather than being asked politely not to look. And the transition judge can return NA when a step restates the problem instead of inferring anything, so setup lines do not count as perfect transitions.

The judge model is Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, later Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, 4-bit quantized, run with sampling off so scores are deterministic.

My writing

03

Result

Total traces generated were 150, that is 30 questions by 5 traces per question. Of those traces, 42 were wrong answer, but 15 of them, 36%, were format failures. That is, the reasoning trace reached the correct final value, computed 70,000 in one case, but wrote #### 7.0 as the final answer.

I started with the 7B Qwen as the judge, but found that it scored 10 out of 10 on 99% of the transitions. In one trace the model generated "7 days in a week" when the problem said 3 times a week. The 7B judge wrote "unsourced, but commonly known" and gave it 7 out of 10.

Therefore I switched to the 14B judge, which scored 0 out of 10 on the same 7 days a week statement, correctly identifying the wrong reasoning. So I went ahead with the 14B judge.

04

Interpretation

The CIs overlap. At n = 27 broken traces it is not feasible to rank the scorers. However each judge performs better than pure chance.

Because of the previously mentioned scale, 0 to 10, and band problem, the within outcome variance also didn't go our way, since our transition judge never used the scale properly.

The fix was to use a 0 to 1 scale instead, with no bands. This was only tested on 30 traces as of now.

Added with Claude · where that explanation runs out

Stated as a hypothesis rather than a cause, because the evidence only partly supports it. Removing the bands raised the spread, 86% down to 64% on the modal value, so the bands were doing something. But the pile-up did not go away, and the unbanded test ran on 30 traces while the banded run used 138, so the comparison is across different problems and not a clean substitution.

The stronger objection is that the isolated judge is also banded and has the widest spread of any scorer. So bands cannot be the whole story. What separates isolated from the rest is that it never sees the prior chain. That suggests a different reading: given the full chain, most individual moves genuinely do follow, so a judge asked "does this follow" answers yes almost always. If that is right, broken transitions are a rare event on GSM8K and the ceiling is a property of the task rather than a defect in the prompt.

Written with Claude · from decisions I made during the work

05

What I would try next

More dimensions in the validity definition. The current definition has three parts and most steps satisfy all three, which is part of why the scores bunch at the top. A finer definition would give the judge somewhere to put a step that is valid but weak, and that headroom is what within outcome variance needs.

Accept that broken transitions are rare, at least on GSM8K. If most moves are fine, the information sits in the tail rather than in the average. That points at counting failures instead of averaging scores, and at picking a harder dataset where local breaks are more common.

More questions. Everything here rests on 27 genuinely broken traces. At the observed rate of roughly 30% wrong answers, of which about two thirds are real reasoning failures, reaching 85 broken traces needs somewhere near 450 generations. That is the cheapest way to make any ranking claim possible at all.

Run the unbanded scorers on the full set. The 0 to 1 result is from 30 traces and cannot be compared directly with the 138 trace banded run.

How I worked with Claude

I used an attempt first rule. Reading the literature, forming the hypothesis, writing the claim, designing the experiment, writing code, interpreting results: I tried each part myself before showing it to a model. Only after I had attempted something a few times and hit a threshold did I hand it over. The threshold was usually one of: days without progress, not knowing what to do next, not being able to write a piece of code or read a result, or being too tired to keep attacking it alone.

Then I would put my attempt in front of Claude to be critiqued, take the critique back, and sharpen it myself. After a few rounds of that, I was often the one catching Claude's mistakes rather than the other way round.

Claude's note on that last sentence. It splits by type rather than by count. Harsh caught the conceptual and design errors: that asking the judge for a score before its reasoning produced confident rationalisation after the fact, that "wrong final answer" does not mean "broken reasoning", that the real failure in the 7 days a week case was contradiction rather than an unsourced value, and that a scorer with no stated criteria matching one with criteria was the result rather than a failure. Claude caught the technical and statistical ones: parsing crashes, truncated traces, that the confidence intervals overlapped when only point estimates had been looked at, and that format failures had never been excluded from any analysis. So Harsh was more reliable about what the work should be, Claude about whether the machinery was sound.

What did not get delegated was the conceptual layer. Every decision that changed what this experiment measures, the claim, the prompts, the scoring rules, what counts as a broken trace, and what would count as failure, was written before a model saw it.

Written with Claude · earlier work this builds on

07

Where this came from

An earlier version of TAPR was built during a hackathon and trained the reward rather than validating it. Qwen2.5-3B was fine tuned with GRPO on GSM8K using a live 7B judge, with a transition reward mixed into the outcome reward.

The one clean result was mechanical. Outcome only training left about half of all problem groups with zero reward variance, which means zero advantage and no learning signal from those problems. Adding the transition reward drove that to near zero on both datasets tested. The accuracy results were weaker: in-domain GSM8K accuracy moved by less than half a point, and a cross-task gap on StrategyQA came from a single seed with a confidence interval that includes zero.

That is why this checkpoint exists. The training run could not tell whether the signal was any good, only that adding something dense changed the optimization. The write-up of that earlier work, with the results marked by how much they can be trusted, is here.
